# LOW STYRENE SBR PATENT LANDSCAPE: EMULSION, SOLUTION, AND MODIFIED BINDER ROUTES

## 1. ABSTRACT
This report synthesizes seven primary patents relevant to low styrene styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) and related styrene-butadiene copolymers. The target is defined as SBR materials with styrene content below conventional SBR levels, typically butadiene-rich rubber/elastomer grades used in tires, tread compounds, and functional binders. Across the selected patents, the landscape spans both solution polymerization and emulsion polymerization, with several documents emphasizing downstream functionalization, oil extension, or binder architecture rather than only base copolymer synthesis. The solution-polymerized disclosures (e.g., tapered or modified SBR in hydrocarbon solvent) use organolithium initiation, hydrocarbon solvents, and post-polymerization quench/coagulation or solvent removal, enabling control over molecular weight and sequence distribution. The emulsion-polymerized disclosures rely on water as the polymerization medium, typically with rosin/fatty-acid soaps, redox initiator systems, mercaptan molecular-weight control, and in some cases reactive emulsifiers or epoxy-acrylate monomers to introduce polarity or silica affinity. Several patents also include post-polymerization water steps such as coagulation, washing, stripping, or extraction, which are important for isolating the rubber or binder. A recurring formulation trend is the use of these low-styrene SBRs in silica- or carbon-black-filled tire tread compounds, where the patents report improved wet grip, wear resistance, processability, or reduced oil demand. Another major trend is tailoring the styrene fraction and microstructure: one patent claims a tapered SBR with 20-30 wt% styrene and 70-80 wt% butadiene, while others disclose emulsion SBRs with styrene around 26-27 wt% or 15-50 wt% depending on functionalization strategy. Overall, the selected patents show that low-styrene SBR development is driven by balancing rolling resistance, wear, and processability through controlled monomer feed, polymerization medium, and post-treatment/compounding design.

## 3. CROSS-PATENT COMPARISON & SYNTHESIS TRENDS
- US11591428B2 and US12037432B2 are solution-polymerized SBR routes in hydrocarbon solvent, while US8530017B2, US9328176B2, EP2986647B1, and US12129321B2 are aqueous emulsion/latex systems.
- The lowest explicit styrene level in the selected set is EP2986647B1 Example 6 with 21.0 wt% bound styrene; US11591428B2 claims 20-30 wt% styrene and examples at 25.7-26.6 wt%; US12037432B2 examples are around 27.0 wt% styrene.
- US8530017B2 and US9328176B2 both use reactive or epoxy-functional monomers in emulsion SBR to improve silica affinity, whereas EP2986647B1 focuses on oil extension with vegetable-oil-based extender oils.
- US12129321B2 differs from the tire-tread-focused patents by targeting a porous cross-linked SBR microsphere binder for lithium-ion batteries, but it still uses aqueous emulsion polymerization and explicit styrene/butadiene feed ratios.
- Water is absent from the core reaction medium in the solution patents US11591428B2 and US12037432B2, but it is central to polymerization and downstream isolation in the emulsion patents, especially coagulation, washing, stripping, and extraction steps.
- Initiator systems vary: organolithium in the solution patents versus redox/peroxide systems in the emulsion patents; mercaptans are used in several emulsion systems and also in the battery-binder patent for molecular-weight control.

## 4. CONCLUSION
Across the selected primary patents, low-styrene SBR is realized through two main manufacturing families: hydrocarbon-solution polymerization with organolithium initiation and aqueous emulsion polymerization with redox/peroxide initiation. The most explicit low-styrene definitions appear in US11591428B2 (20-30 wt% styrene, 70-80 wt% butadiene) and EP2986647B1 (21.0 wt% bound styrene in Example 6), while US12037432B2 and the emulsion patents cluster around the mid-20 wt% styrene range. Several patents further tune performance by introducing reactive emulsifiers, epoxy-acrylate functionality, or oil extension, and by using coagulation/washing or solvent-removal workups to isolate the rubber. Collectively, these disclosures show that low-styrene SBR development is driven by balancing filler compatibility, wear resistance, processability, and downstream compound performance rather than by styrene reduction alone.
