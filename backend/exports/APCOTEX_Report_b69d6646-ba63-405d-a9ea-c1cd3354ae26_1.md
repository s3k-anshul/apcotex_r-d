# HNBR SYNTHESIS, HYDROGENATION, AND DEGRADATION PROCESS LANDSCAPE FROM SELECTED EP/US PATENTS

## 1. ABSTRACT
This report synthesizes nine primary EP/US patents centered on hydrogenated nitrile butadiene rubber (HNBR) and closely related manufacturing routes. The selected landscape spans direct hydrogenation of NBR to HNBR, continuous hydrogenation in multistage reactors, low-molecular-weight HNBR formation by simultaneous metathesis/hydrogenation, and metathetic degradation of nitrile rubbers as a precursor step to lower-viscosity HNBR. Across the set, the target material is consistently defined by hydrogenation of the diene-derived unsaturation in nitrile rubber backbones, with the requested target attribute being the degree of hydrogenation or residual double-bond content. Several patents emphasize catalyst systems based on ruthenium, rhodium, palladium, osmium, or Grubbs/Hoveyda/Grela-type metathesis complexes, while others focus on process intensification through spinning disk reactors or multistage agitated continuous reactors. Organic solvents dominate the disclosed media, especially monochlorobenzene and chlorobenzene, although one patent uses aqueous emulsion polymerization to make the NBR precursor before coagulation and drying. Water is therefore present in some patents, but usually as a polymerization medium for precursor NBR or as workup/coagulation/washing rather than as the HNBR hydrogenation medium itself. Formulation/process trends include low catalyst loadings in ppm or phr, hydrogen pressures from a few bar to tens of bar, temperatures commonly around 100–140°C, and reaction times ranging from minutes in continuous systems to hours in batch systems. Several patents report residual double-bond levels, hydrogenation percentages, molecular-weight reduction, and improved aging or processability as the key performance outcomes. Overall, the portfolio shows two dominant technical directions: catalyst/process optimization for efficient hydrogenation of NBR to HNBR, and precursor degradation/metathesis strategies to access lower-molecular-weight HNBR with narrower distributions and improved handling.

## 3. CROSS-PATENT COMPARISON & SYNTHESIS TRENDS
- Hydrogenation-focused patents (EP3827029A1, US7666950B2, EP2473281B1, US9079979B2, EP2147933B1) disclose organic-solvent solution processing, most often in monochlorobenzene/chlorobenzene, whereas the precursor-manufacturing patent US9023914B2 uses organic-solvent polymerization for inventive NBR and water only in comparative emulsion examples.
- Direct HNBR hydrogenation patents report explicit hydrogenation metrics: US2147933B1 gives 61-99.4% hydrogenation levels, US7666950B2 reports residual double bonds from 0.9% to 25.4%, and EP2473281B1/US9079979B2 report RDB values down to 0.4% and 0.3% respectively.
- Catalyst systems differ substantially: EP3827029A1 centers on Ru/Pd/Rh hydrogenation catalysts; US7666950B2 and US9079979B2 use Grubbs/Hoveyda/Wilkinson-type catalysts in spinning-disk batch hydrogenation; EP2147933B1 uses osmium catalyst precursor in continuous flow; EP2473281B1 uses Ru/Os catalysts for simultaneous hydrogenation-metathesis.
- Process intensification is a major trend: US7666950B2 uses a spinning disk reactor, EP2147933B1 uses a multistage agitated continuous reactor, while EP2473281B1 and US9079979B2 combine hydrogenation with metathesis to reduce molecular weight in a single step.
- The degradation patents US8362154B2 and US9593168B2 do not directly report HNBR hydrogenation degree, but they show that catalyst choice and 1-hexene addition strongly affect molecular-weight reduction of NBR precursors, which is relevant to downstream HNBR manufacturing.

## 4. CONCLUSION
Across the selected primary patents, HNBR is predominantly produced by solution-phase hydrogenation of NBR using noble-metal catalysts, with the degree of hydrogenation controlled by catalyst identity, catalyst loading, pressure, temperature, and reactor design. The most directly informative patents report either residual double-bond content or hydrogenation percentage, showing that near-complete hydrogenation is achievable under optimized conditions, while lower catalyst loading or less favorable operating conditions leave substantially higher residual unsaturation. A second major theme is precursor tailoring by metathesis or controlled degradation to lower molecular weight before or during hydrogenation, enabling lower-viscosity HNBR grades. Water is generally absent from the HNBR hydrogenation medium and appears mainly in precursor emulsion polymerization or downstream workup in non-primary contexts.
