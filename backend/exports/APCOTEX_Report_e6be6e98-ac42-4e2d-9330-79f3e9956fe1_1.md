# LOW-STYRENE SBR-RELATED COPOLYMERIZATION AND RUBBER COMPOSITION PATENTS

## 1. ABSTRACT
This report reviews two primary patents relevant to low-styrene styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) synthesis and formulation, focusing on how styrene incorporation, microstructure, and blending strategy are controlled to tune tire-relevant properties. Although neither patent is a classic low-styrene emulsion SBR disclosure, both address composition/process design in styrene-butadiene-based elastomers where styrene content and distribution are central to performance. US10759885B2 discloses solution polymerization of branched conjugated diene/vinyl copolymers followed by optional hydrogenation, with examples using butadiene/styrene and branched dienes such as myrcene and farnesene; the patent emphasizes processability, bleeding suppression, and tire tread performance through hydrogenated branched copolymers blended into SBR-containing rubber compositions. US11549003B2 discloses a solution SSBR architecture with two parts of different vinyl content, prepared by adiabatic temperature-rise polymerization in cyclohexane using n-butyllithium, randomizing agents, and delayed monomer addition to suppress styrene block formation while maintaining high aromatic vinyl content and compatibility with BR or NR. Across the landscape, the dominant synthesis approach is solution polymerization rather than emulsion polymerization, with strong emphasis on monomer feed sequencing, randomization control, and post-polymer modification. Key formulation trends include blending with high-cis BR or natural rubber, use of silica/silane reinforcement systems, and targeting a balance of wet grip, rolling resistance, abrasion resistance, and processability. For low-styrene SBR specifically, the evidence suggests that practical routes rely less on simply lowering styrene to very low levels and more on controlling styrene distribution, block suppression, and compatibility through microstructure engineering and blending. Recommended synthesis parameters from the evidence include controlled monomer feed ratios, low block styrene formation, moderate molecular weight, and solution polymerization with initiator/randomizer systems that enable composition gradients or segmented microstructures.

## 3. CROSS-PATENT COMPARISON & SYNTHESIS TRENDS
- Both patents use solution polymerization rather than emulsion polymerization, with anionic initiator chemistry centered on n-butyllithium.
- US10759885B2 focuses on branched diene/vinyl copolymers and hydrogenation, while US11549003B2 focuses on segmented SSBR microstructure with two parts of different vinyl content.
- Styrene content in US10759885B2 examples is typically 90 g styrene with 110 g butadiene or 125 g styrene with branched dienes, whereas US11549003B2 claims a higher aromatic vinyl content of 38 to 42 wt% in the copolymer.
- US10759885B2 uses TMEDA in at least one example and THF in others; US11549003B2 uses DTHFP, piperidine, and 1-(4-vinylbenzyl)pyrrolidine as randomizing/modifying agents.
- US10759885B2 includes hydrogenation at 5.0 kgf/cm2 and 80° C. to 50% hydrogenation; US11549003B2 does not disclose hydrogenation but instead uses delayed monomer addition and silane coupling-agent termination.
- US11549003B2 explicitly controls block-like composition by adding 1,3-butadiene when the temperature rises 30° C. from initiation, whereas US10759885B2 emphasizes complete polymerization and optional post-hydrogenation.
- For formulation, both patents target tire compounds and use silica/silane or rubber blending strategies, but US11549003B2 more explicitly compares compatibility with high-cis BR and natural rubber.

## 4. CONCLUSION
The evidence indicates that low-styrene SBR development is best approached through solution-polymerization microstructure control rather than simple styrene reduction alone. US10759885B2 shows that styrene-containing copolymers can be tuned through branched diene selection and hydrogenation to improve processability and tire performance, while US11549003B2 demonstrates that segmented SSBRs with controlled vinyl gradients and delayed monomer addition can manage compatibility and performance even at relatively high styrene levels. For low-styrene synthesis, the most transferable parameters are controlled monomer feed ratio, suppression of styrene block formation, use of randomizing agents/modifiers, and molecular-weight control in the 100,000 to 250,000 range where disclosed. The strongest evidence supports solution polymerization with n-BuLi-based initiation, staged monomer addition, and careful control of vinyl content and block distribution as the preferred route to styrene-butadiene elastomers with improved processability and balanced tire properties.
